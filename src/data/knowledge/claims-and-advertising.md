# FSSAI — Health Claims, Nutrient Claims and Advertising

## Permitted claims

Claims on food labels and in advertising must be truthful, clear, unambiguous, and must not
mislead the consumer. The FSS (Health Claims, Advertising and Claims) Regulations govern which
claims are permitted. Only nutrition claims listed as permitted may be used, and they must meet
the prescribed conditions.

## Nutrient content claims

Common permitted nutrient claims and their conditions:

- "Low fat": not more than 3 g fat per 100 g solid food, or 1.5 g per 100 ml liquid.
- "Fat free": not more than 0.5 g fat per 100 g or 100 ml.
- "Sugar free": not more than 0.5 g sugars per 100 g or 100 ml, with a statement that it contains
  naturally occurring sugars if applicable.
- "No added sugar": no sugar or sugar-containing ingredient added during processing.
- "Low sodium": not more than 120 mg sodium per 100 g or 100 ml.
- "High fibre": not less than 6 g dietary fibre per 100 g, or 3 g per 100 kcal.
- "Reduced": at least 25% less of the named nutrient compared with the reference product.

## Health claims

A health claim describes a relationship between a food or ingredient and a health effect (e.g.,
"calcium helps build strong bones"). Only claims scientifically substantiated and recognised by
the regulations may be used. Claims must not create a perception of risk in the absence of the
food, must not exaggerate, and must not suggest that the food can prevent, treat or cure a
disease — such medicinal claims are prohibited.

## Comparative and other claims

Comparative claims (e.g., "30% less sugar than X") must compare like-for-like foods and identify
the comparator. Claims about vitamins and minerals require the nutrient to be present at a
declared minimum level. Functional claims about probiotics, omega-3, etc., are allowed only when
the food meets the prescribed nutrient conditions and the claim wording is approved.

## Advertising restrictions

Advertising must not disparage competitors, must not mislead by omission, and must not be
targeted in a way that exploits children's lack of experience. Products cannot advertise
therapeutic or medicinal properties. Manufacturers must be able to substantiate every claim made,
and the Food Authority may require substantiation at any time.

## Consequences

Making prohibited or misleading claims is an offence of misbranding/unsafe food advertisement
under the Act and attracts penalties, including fines and, for repeat offenders, imprisonment.
